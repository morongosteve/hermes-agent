# Analogy & Conceptual Blending

**One line:** Map the structure of one domain onto another to break a frame, or blend two inputs into a third that has properties of neither alone.

**Invented by:** Analogy/structure-mapping — Dedre Gentner (1983). Conceptual blending (the "fourth space") — Gilles Fauconnier & Mark Turner (*The Way We Think*, 2002). Kin: Koestler's "bisociation" (*The Act of Creation*, 1964).

## When to use

- **Any** domain when you need to break a frame, find a generative metaphor, or fuse two things into something new.
- You're stuck inside one way of seeing the problem and need a foreign structure to import.

## Two moves

### Analogy (structure-mapping)
Creativity by analogy maps **relations**, not surface features. The power is matching the *system of relationships*: "an atom is like a solar system" works because *central body / orbiting bodies / attractive force* maps across, not because they look alike. To use it: find a **source domain** you understand well whose *relational structure* matches your target, then port the relations and see what they predict.

### Blending (the fourth space)
Take two input "spaces", find a **generic space** of what they share, then build a **blend** that inherits selectively from both and develops **emergent structure** neither had. "Houseboat" blends house + boat; the blend has emergent properties (you can live mobile on water) belonging to neither parent. Good blends run on their own logic once built.

## The method

1. **Name the target** — the problem/thing you're reframing.
2. **Pick a source** (for analogy) or a **second input** (for blend). Choose by *relational* resemblance, or deliberately distant for a blend with more emergent surprise.
3. **Map the correspondences.** What plays the role of what? Where does the mapping hold, and — crucially — **where does it break?** The break is often the insight.
4. **Project and develop.** Carry the source's relations/structure into the target; for a blend, run the new space's own logic and harvest the emergent properties.
5. **Keep the live parts.** Drop the mapping once it's given you the idea; don't defend the metaphor past its usefulness.

## Worked example

Target: redesign how a team does code review (feels adversarial, slow).
- Source (analogy): **a writers' workshop.** Relations ported: *author reads intent aloud → peers respond to what's working first → critique is about the work, not the person → the author decides what to accept.*
- Where it breaks: code has objective failure modes a poem doesn't — so keep the "respond to intent + strengths first" relation, drop the "all feedback is subjective" one.
- Blend angle: **code review × air-crash investigation** → emergent structure: blameless, focused on systemic causes, producing a reusable "finding", not a verdict on the pilot. That emergent "blameless findings" property (from neither parent alone) becomes the new review norm.

## Output shape

Name the source/second input, lay out the mapping (including where it breaks), and state the idea or emergent property it produced. The break-point is usually the most valuable part — surface it.
