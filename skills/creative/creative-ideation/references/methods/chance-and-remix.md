# Chance & Remix

**One line:** Introduce randomness and found material — cut-ups, decks, dice, sampling — and recombine it into something you couldn't have planned.

**Invented by:** Dada (Tristan Tzara's "To Make a Dadaist Poem", 1920); the cut-up method (Brion Gysin & William S. Burroughs, 1950s–60s); John Cage's chance operations via the *I Ching*; hip-hop sampling and musique concrète.

## When to use

- TEXT or OBJECT domain when you have **source material to remix** (a draft, a corpus, samples, found images).
- UNBLOCKING via randomness when `oblique-strategies` isn't enough and you want raw recombination.

## The idea

Your intentions are the thing keeping you at the center. Chance operations route *around* intention: the juxtapositions randomness produces are ones your taste would have vetoed, and some of those are exactly the fresh ones. Found material carries meaning you didn't author, which you then curate — the creative act shifts from *making* to *selecting and framing*.

## Techniques

- **Cut-up** — take a text (yours or found), cut it into fragments, rearrange at random, read for the lines that suddenly work. ("When you cut into the present the future leaks out." — Burroughs.)
- **Fold-in** — fold two pages together so columns from each meet; read across the seam.
- **Deck of prompts** — put constraints/words/images on cards; draw to combine.
- **Dice / random tables** — assign options to numbers; roll to choose, committing to the result.
- **Sampling** — lift a fragment (a phrase, a loop, a texture) out of its context into yours; the friction between old and new context is the content.
- **Exquisite corpse** — each contributor adds blind to what came before.

## The method

1. **Choose a source** rich enough to surprise you (not your own tidy outline — something with texture).
2. **Apply a chance operation** — commit to the randomness; don't pre-curate.
3. **Read/scan for the hits** — the 2–3 juxtapositions that land. This selection is where your taste re-enters.
4. **Frame and develop** the hits into finished material. The randomness is scaffolding; the framing is yours.

## Worked example

Stuck on a band bio (reads like a press release).
- Source: the band's own group-chat + a weather report.
- Cut-up + fold-in produces the line *"small craft advisory for four people who rehearse in a garage that floods."*
- That accidental image — talent + precarity + literal flooding — becomes the bio's spine. A planned draft never writes that sentence.

## Output shape

Show the raw chance output (at least a glimpse), mark the hits you selected, then the developed result. The visible randomness teaches the move.
