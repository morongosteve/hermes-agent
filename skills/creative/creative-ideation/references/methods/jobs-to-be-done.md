# Jobs To Be Done

**One line:** People don't buy products, they "hire" them to make progress in a specific situation; design for the *job*, not the demographic.

**Invented by:** Clayton Christensen & collaborators (the milkshake study; *Competing Against Luck*, 2016). Related strands: Tony Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation, Bob Moesta's "forces" framing.

## When to use

- PRODUCT domain: business, service, feature, go-to-market framing.
- On high-slop product terrain, pair with `lateral-provocations` to break the obvious solution shape after framing the real job.

## The idea

A "job" is the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance — functional, emotional, and social. Customers "hire" a product to get that job done and "fire" it when something does the job better. The famous case: a chain discovered a surprising share of milkshakes were "hired" for the *morning commute* — to make a boring drive less dull and stave off hunger till lunch. Competitors weren't other shakes; they were bananas and bagels. That reframing changed the product (thicker, so it lasts the commute) far more than demographic data ever could.

## The method

1. **Find the struggling moment.** When did the customer last switch to or seek out something? What were they trying to accomplish right then?
2. **Describe the job as a situation + motivation + outcome:** "When I [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]." Keep it solution-agnostic.
3. **Map the four forces** on the switch:
   - **Push** of the current situation (what's bad now)
   - **Pull** of the new solution (what's attractive)
   - **Anxiety** about the new (what makes them hesitate)
   - **Habit** of the present (inertia)
   A switch happens only when push + pull beats anxiety + habit. Design to raise the first pair and lower the second.
4. **Identify the real competitors** — everything else "hired" for this job, including non-consumption and duct-tape workarounds.
5. **Design for the job**, then (optionally) provoke the solution shape with `lateral-provocations`.

## Worked example

A B2B note-taking tool with flat growth.
- Struggling moment: users sign up before a big meeting, abandon after.
- Job: *"When I'm about to walk into a high-stakes meeting, I want to look prepared and not forget anything, so I come across as on top of it."* (Note the social/emotional core — not "store notes.")
- Forces: Push = fear of looking unprepared; Pull = structured prep; **Anxiety** = "another tool to learn before the meeting" (the killer); Habit = a notepad.
- Design implication: a 30-second, zero-learning "meeting prep" flow that works the first time, killing the anxiety force. The competitor was the paper notepad and ChatGPT, not other note apps.

## Output shape

The job statement (situation/motivation/outcome), the four forces, the true competitor set, and the design implication that follows from the dominant force.

## Pairs well with

`lateral-provocations` (break the obvious solution once the job is framed), `analogy-and-blending` (port a business model from another industry serving the same job).
